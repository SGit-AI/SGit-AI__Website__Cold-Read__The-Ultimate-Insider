## 2. What is the claim

Citations in this section are to `src/static/talk/talk.json` at `298be4e`, by beat `id` and the line that `id` sits on; each quote is from that beat's `say`, the words the speaker says.

**In one sentence, as I would put it to a colleague:** an agent is an insider with none of the limits that used to cap insiders, it is being dropped into infrastructure and permissions that were never built to hold it, and the people who should decide about it are too slow; the fix is to measure what it can reach, contain it with real boundaries, license it, and let it earn autonomy, and that fix is a business opportunity.

The talk builds that in five moves.

**1. A threat does not need a villain.** "A threat is anything that causes business impact. It does not need to be malicious." (`definition`, line 64). This is the hinge of the whole talk, and the one move I fully accept: most of the agent damage I have seen was an agent being diligent, not hostile.

**2. Agents lost the limits that made insiders manageable.** "Skill. Motivation. Fatigue. Fear of getting caught. And stopping once the objective was reached. Each of those worked as a control that nobody designed. Agents have none of them." (`limits`, line 139). The vivid version: "An attacker chooses, and stops. An agent turns right. It goes where the task points, and it keeps going." (`building`, line 186). And the evidence is five incidents, of which "Three of those five were not attacks. They were agents being agents. The distance between the two is a prompt." (`incidents`, line 220).

**3. The inside cannot hold them.** "The outside is in balance ... The inside is where nobody looked." (`inside`, line 270). Grants are far wider than use: "workload identities use less than 5 per cent of the permissions granted to them" (`permissions`, line 311), so "its grant is not what you meant. It is everything the role ever accumulated." Recovery is too coarse: "most operations are edits, not appends, and backups run by the day, not by the transaction" (`infrastructure`, line 340). And the new failure is integrity: "A thief takes a copy. An agent changes the original." (`integrity`, line 373).

**4. Risk management is too slow, so the default answer is the off switch.** "48 per cent of organisations track risk in them [spreadsheets]. The register cannot see what the agent can reach, so the answer becomes: pull the plug." (`spreadsheets`, line 418).

**5. The way out is containment, and containment buys speed.** Four moves: "Measure what the agent can actually reach. Write down what stands in the way. Give it a licence to operate that can be suspended. And let it earn its autonomy." (`way-out`, line 452). The sharpest single claim, and the one I would steal for my own team: "a boundary, a setting, an expectation, or nothing. Only a boundary is a control. Most of what we call controls today are expectations" (`abp`, line 464). The close turns the whole thing round: "The more you can constrain an agent, the more you can trust it, and the more autonomy you can afford to give it." (`brakes`, line 838).

**What the claim quietly rests on, which a sceptic will notice.**

- *The way out has product names on it.* The four moves are presented through named tools: "This is what RiskMandate's Agent Behaviour Policy writes down" (`abp`, line 464), then a run of ten beats on SHarness (`s-sharness-one-idea`, line 623, to `s-sharness`, line 731), then "It is all on sgit.ai." (`live-sgit-demos`, line 791). Who builds RiskMandate, SHarness and sgit.ai, and whether the speaker does: the talk's words do not say. The home page names him as "founder of The Cyber Boardroom" (`src/pages/index.html`, line 8, at `298be4e`), a fourth name. For a sales-sensitive reader that is the first question, and the record I read does not answer it.
- *Part of the way out is still a promise.* "the suspension is what we are building towards" (`licence`, line 569); SHarness has a beat titled "what exists and what is coming" (`s-sharness-components`, line 647). The talk is honest about this, which I credit; it also means the strongest move, a licence the system suspends by itself, does not exist yet by the talk's own account.
- *The proof of "order and structure" is the talk's own making.* "This is what agentic work will look like: order and structure. ... The capability is the orchestration, not the model." (`team`, line 406), with "510 commits and 164 releases ... and not one hold" (`m03`, line 22). That is a claim about one night and one team, by the team; how "not one hold" was counted, the talk's words do not say.
- *The argument is not "agents will be weaponised".* The talk goes out of its way to say attackers are slow because "attackers are businesses" (`no-worm-era`, line 160) and the danger is ours: "We give them the brief, the access and the motivation ourselves." That is the part I did not expect, and the part I find most honest.

## 3. Is it convincing

Mostly yes on the diagnosis, not yet on the cure. The test I applied: for every number or incident the speaker says, does the site show me where it came from? Two places answer that at `298be4e`: each beat's `sources` list in `src/static/talk/talk.json` (shown on the script page, `talk/script.html`, as each beat's "Sources"), and the site's "Every number on screen, and where it comes from" page (`src/pages/sources/index.html`, line 4). I did not open any of the outside links: the record I may read is the source, so whether each link says what the slide says, the record does not say. What I can judge is whether the claim is traceable, and who is vouching for it.

**What convinced me: the incidents are traceable, and the talk counts honestly.** Every incident in the `incidents` beat carries a named source in `talk.json` (lines 227 to 251: EchoLeak, Replit, Gemini CLI, Amazon Q, the Anthropic espionage report, and Microsoft on Midnight Blizzard). The 82-to-1 identity figure names CyberArk (`identities`, line 294), the permission figures name Microsoft (`permissions`, line 311), the outage names ThousandEyes (`no-worm-era`, line 160, its source at line 171). And the line that makes the argument, "Three of those five were not attacks" (`incidents`, line 220), is one I can check against the list in the same beat: Replit, Gemini CLI and the Amazon Q prompt are accidents or tampering, not an adversary driving an agent. The speaker is not inflating the threat; he is narrowing it, which is why I believe him.

**What I would push back on, as an engineer, in order of weight.**

1. **The sources page is not "every number on screen".** Its title promises that (`src/pages/sources/index.html`, line 4), but the script cites sources the page never lists: the gym-booking agent (ABC News, `talk.json` line 154), Google's threat-intelligence report behind "not yet observed fully autonomous attack pipelines" (line 175), the White House briefing and Microsoft's Storm-0558 analysis behind the SolarWinds and Storm-0558 numbers (lines 197 and 201), Midnight Blizzard (line 251), the High Court judgment behind the fake case law (line 388), the Lovable and Replit user numbers (lines 442 and 445), and the PHP security release behind "open source is about to become very secure" (lines 850 and 854). By my count, 13 of the 32 distinct source links in `talk.json` do not appear on the sources page; two of the 13 are other links for incidents the page does list (Replit, the espionage report). A reader who goes to the sources page, as I would, finds that much of the talk's evidence missing and starts to wonder what else is.
2. **The markdown copy drops the sources.** The script's markdown version says it is "generated from the same content as the page, so the two cannot drift" (`_site/talk/script.md`, line 5, built from `298be4e`), but it carries none of the per-beat sources the HTML page shows: two links in the whole file, against one per source in `script.html`. For a site that offers a "Site index for agents", the copy an agent would read is the one without the evidence.
3. **The growth numbers are the vendors' own.** "Lovable reports over sixty million projects, Replit over fifty million registered users" (`spreadsheets`, line 418). The speaker's cue knows it: "Lovable and Replit figures are vendor-reported: say so if asked." (`talk.json`, line 426). Said aloud, the word "reports" is the only hedge. The 48 per cent spreadsheet figure is second-hand too: a Secureframe survey "as reported by RegScale" (`src/pages/sources/index.html`, line 31). These carry part two, "explorer coding", which is the part closest to my own work.
4. **The way out is sourced to the speaker's side of the table.** The two sources for the four moves are "RiskMandate, Agent Behaviour Policy" and "The Kit Bag plan" on sgit.ai (`talk.json`, `abp`, line 464, and `src/pages/sources/index.html`, lines 41 and 42), and the one number in that section, "thirteen things beyond its purpose. Four were stopped by the platform", comes from that same Kit Bag plan. Part one is evidenced by Microsoft, CyberArk, Google and the courts; part three by the products it recommends. That is not wrong, but it is the point at which the talk stops being a briefing and starts being a pitch, and the record does not show me anyone outside those projects testing them.
5. **The team's own record is self-reported.** "510 commits and 164 releases, 182 of the commits overnight, and not one hold" (`m03`, line 22) has no source in its beat; the `prelude` beat that replays it cites "Team in numbers, from this repository's public records, 17:02 UTC" (line 40), an article on the talk's own site. As a git user I could recount the commits; how a "hold" is defined and counted, the talk does not say.
6. **One claim is anecdote, and is honest about it.** "Most of the worst damage I have seen in twenty-five years was a bug, a misconfiguration, a script run against the wrong environment." (`definition`, line 64). No source, and none needed: it is said as experience. It matches mine.

**Verdict on section 3.** Part one (agents as insiders, the incidents, the permissions) convinced me, and the restraint of "three of those five were not attacks" did more for the speaker's credibility than any statistic. Part two leans on vendor numbers. Part three asks me to trust tools whose only evidence is their makers' own pages, with the strongest piece, a licence the system suspends, still "what we are building towards" (`licence`, line 569). I leave convinced of the problem and interested in the cure, not yet convinced by it.

## 4. What would I pay for the book of it

**There is no book in the record.** I searched the source at `298be4e` for one; the only "book" in `src/` and `content/` is the gym-booking story (`src/static/studio/data/pivoting.json`). The long-form version the talk is "built from" is an article on another site (`src/pages/sources/index.html`, line 5), which I may not read. So this answer prices what a book of *this* talk would be, from what the site shows me.

**The material as it stands: nothing, because it is free and short.** The whole script is 4,141 words (`_site/talk/script.md`, built from `298be4e`), about twenty minutes' reading, and it is all on the site with its sources. I would not pay for a printed copy of the script.

**A book that kept part one and finished part three: about the price of a technical paperback, 30 to 40 pounds, and I would expense it.** Part one is already the best short case I have read for treating my own agents as insiders; its incidents and identity numbers are sourced (section 3). What would make it worth paying for is what the talk leaves to its demos and its sister sites:

- the four moves worked through on an ordinary engineering setup, a repository, a CI pipeline and a coding agent, not only on the speaker's own projects (`abp`, `licence`, `s-e14` in `talk.json`);
- the claim "Only a boundary is a control" (`abp`, line 464) turned into a checklist I can run against my own agent's permissions;
- the agent team of the prelude (`m03`, `prelude`) written up as a case study, failures included, since "without its policies, a team like this goes off-piste just as fast" (`team`, line 406) is asserted, not shown;
- the evidence the sources page leaves out (section 3).

**A book that was the talk plus the product pages: nothing.** If the book's part three is RiskMandate, SHarness and sgit.ai as they appear here, I can read the product pages for free, and I would read the book as a brochure.

As a reader I am told, repeatedly, that the author is not sure nearly half of what I am reading belongs. I did not stop there, but that is where I first wanted to.

**For good, at beat 40, "SHarness: the one idea".** Its whole script is "The one idea behind SHarness." (`s-sharness-one-idea`, line 623). From beat 27, "The way out", to beat 54, the talk runs 28 beats and about 28 minutes (1,670 seconds of the planned 4,495, summed from each beat's `dur` in `talk.json`), and 19 of those 28 beats have fewer than 20 words to say, and ten of the 28 are about SHarness. The first 40 minutes argue; the next 28 show. In the room, with a live kernel in a browser tab (`live-sharness-linux`, line 671), that may be the best part of the evening. On the page, it is where the argument stops and I close the tab: the words that carried me through part one ("A thief takes a copy. An agent changes the original.", `integrity`, line 373) have no equal after beat 39.

**What would have kept me reading.** One paragraph per demo, in the script, saying what I would have seen and what it proves; and the closing beats (`evolution`, `next-companies`, `brakes`) moved up to follow "The way out", so the business case comes before the products and not after.

**A smaller snag, before I even start.** The player opens on a splash that says "74:55 · 58 slides" while its own timer says "3:00 / 90:00" (`_site/talk/player.html#three`, built from `298be4e`; screenshot `debriefs/screenshots/2026-10-30__source-slide-three-298be4e.png`). Two lengths on one screen, for a talk whose subject is measuring what something can actually do.

## In one paragraph

A security talk for security people and would-be founders, in which I, the engineer running the agents, am the subject. Its diagnosis is sharp, honest and sourced: agents are insiders with no natural limits, dropped into infrastructure and permissions that cannot hold them, judged by risk processes too slow to matter. Its cure is promising and, on this record, still its makers' word. I would recommend part one to my team tomorrow, read part three as a demo reel, and pay for the book only if it finished what the demos start.
